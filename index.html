Are Australia's Frogs Disappearing?

Thousands of people record frogs on their phones.
What do records tell us, and what do they hide?

Frogs live in rainforests and wetlands all over the world, including Malaysia, where many of us hear them after the rain. Many frogs need both water and land, so they react quickly when their home changes. That is one reason scientists watch frogs closely.

This page looks at Australia, where records of frogs and a national list of threatened frogs are both open to the public. Do the records show that frogs are doing well, or is the picture more fragile?

How much have threatened frogs declined?

Scientists follow 28 types of threatened or near-threatened frogs, mostly in eastern Australia.

Between 1985 and 2022, their numbers fell by about 97%, on average.

Source: Threatened Species Index (2025).

Australia keeps an official list of species at risk of extinction. It names 53 frog types (species or subspecies), which we call listed frogs. Four are already extinct.

Can sightings tell us the story?

FrogID is a phone app made by the Australian Museum. Since 2017 it has collected more than a million records, but more records do not always mean more frogs: more people can take part.

Source: Australian Museum (n.d.-c); Fitzgerald (2020); Rowley and Callaghan (2020).

Records cluster around big cities such as Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane. This may partly reflect where people use FrogID.

The two largest circles, around Sydney and Melbourne, hold more than a third of the records shown.

Each circle covers an area about 300 kilometres across.
Source: Australian Museum (n.d.-c, 2024); Natural Earth (n.d.).

Between 2018 and 2023, Victoria's share rose from 12% to 21%, while Western Australia and the Northern Territory lost more than half of theirs.

This may reflect where people record, rather than where frogs live.

Which frogs call when?

Records depend on the season as well as on the frogs. In most states they peak in November, in spring.

Western Australia peaks in August and the Northern Territory in January.

Source: Australian Museum (n.d.-b, n.d.-c); Bureau of Meteorology (n.d.-a, n.d.-b).

Nearly half of all FrogID records are made in spring (September to November), which can hide differences between frogs.

The frogs fall into three groups: winter, spring and summer callers.

Each bar marks months with more records than usual, once the number of people recording in that month is taken into account.
A frog with two bars has two separate periods.

What does it mean?

Are Australia's frogs disappearing? For the threatened frogs that scientists follow, the answer is yes: their numbers have fallen sharply. For frogs in general, records alone cannot tell us.

Listed frogs are not spread evenly. Their records are concentrated in the south-east, and two species make up most of them.

Sightings are useful, but they reflect both the frogs and where and when people record. Counting frogs carefully, as the Threatened Frog Index does, is what shows the decline.

This is worth remembering in Malaysia too: records from many people are valuable, but their number alone does not show whether frogs are doing well.
